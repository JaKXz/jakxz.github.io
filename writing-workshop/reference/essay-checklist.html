Reference · Argumentative technical essays

Steal the engine, not the paint

Borrow the machinery that makes a writer clear. Keep the details that make a reader recognize you.

Borrow this structure

  • □One position: say what you believe, not only what happened.
  • □Early orientation: let the opening reveal the argument.
  • □Concrete evidence: use a real incident with consequential details.
  • □Causal links: explain why the example supports the claim.
  • □Real objections: answer the strongest likely disagreement.
  • □Dense edges: make the beginning and ending carry meaning.

Derived from Sean Goedecke’s own descriptions of his writing practice and argument-driven blogging.

Keep this voice

  • □Your scenes: Sunday morning, illness, dread, and the work waiting Monday.
  • □Your cadence: short declarations beside longer explanatory sentences.
  • □Your humor: church language, asides, and rhetorical questions.
  • □Your judgment: principles about tools, reviewability, and deterministic leverage.
  • □Your artifacts: commands, graphs, aliases, and the details you actually used.

The section test

  1. What claim does this section advance?
  2. What evidence, mechanism, or objection does it contribute?
  3. Could it disappear without weakening the thesis?
  4. Does it sound like something you would actually say?

If the first two answers are unclear, restructure it. If the third answer is yes, cut it. If the fourth answer is no, rewrite it from memory instead of editing the borrowed sentence.

Ask the machine last

  1. Revise the argument and read the prose aloud first.
  2. Run vale src/lib/posts/i-attended-the-church-of-jj.md.
  3. Classify each alert: actionable, intentional voice, or vocabulary.
  4. Change a sentence only when you can explain what becomes clearer.

Vale can notice patterns consistently. It cannot decide whether “Very.” is a weak intensifier or the right dry answer to a rhetorical question. That judgment is the writer’s job.
